# Concept development

Use when inventing a premise, hook or treatment, or when the current idea feels predictable.
Start from the user's brief and selected personality. Approved narration and supplied wording remain
fixed unless rewriting is requested. This is working guidance, not another production stage.

## Find the central tension

Name the intended viewer, what they want to understand or experience, and the obstacle that makes
that interesting. Separate evidence from assumptions about the audience. State the video's promise
in one sentence; choose a concept that can deliver it within the requested scope.

Explore materially different approaches when useful:

- Inversion: what if the expected safe action causes the danger, or the apparent solution is the problem?
- Subtraction: what familiar element could be removed to expose the mechanism or create unease?
- Combination: can an unrelated situation make the idea easier to see without distorting its meaning?

There is no required idea count. Describe candidates concretely enough to distinguish their premise,
payoff and likely pictures; changing adjectives does not create another concept.

## Rules-story

Develop a specific anomaly, a counterintuitive rule, the consequence of breaking it and a twist that
reinterprets an earlier detail. Test causal logic: why this rule, why this consequence, and what can the
viewer notice before the reveal? For example, a lift displays the occupant's destination before they
press anything; the instruction must follow from the story's invented mechanism, not random menace.

Respect the identity's locked opening, title formula, ending and series frame. Invent original lore;
never present fictional survival rules as verified advice. Avoid recycling a previous video's premise.

## Kai-explainer

Choose a central question with a sourced reason to care. Explore different hooks and an original
visual analogy that can develop through the explanation. Identify where the analogy stops matching
the real mechanism; show or state the limitation before it creates a false inference.

For example, a queue may explain waiting for limited compute, but does not by itself explain how a
model reasons. Do not let an appealing metaphor supply an unsupported technical claim. A novel
angle still needs the evidence, truthful narrator experience and payoff required by the writing brief.

## Challenge the choice

Argue against the preferred idea: what is obvious, confusing, unsupported, difficult to picture or
unlikely to pay off? Compare it with known work when relevant, but do not treat a finite reference
library as proof of originality. Revise the weak point or choose another approach.

Record only useful decisions in existing project notes, VISUALS.md or the explainer's PACKAGE.md:
the chosen promise, premise or analogy, its limits, and why the rejected alternative was weaker.
No Cannes scores, numerical quality threshold or mandatory refinement loop. These notes do not
replace independent script or film review.
